How to Keep Track of Rows and Repeats When Knitting or Crocheting

By Will McCullough, DigiRune Studios · November 23, 2026

The short answer: pick one place where your count lives, and update it the moment you finish a row, not later. For most projects that means a pencil mark on a printed pattern or a row counter, plus stitch markers for repeats inside a row. For long or tricky work, add a lifeline every so often, and learn to count rows in the fabric itself so you can always check your count against your knitting.

Losing your place is a frustration knitters and crocheters talk about often, and it is not only a beginner problem. You put the work down for dinner, come back, and cannot tell if you just finished row 22 or row 23. Knitters on the KnittingHelp forum have traded methods for this for years, and the good news is that the best ones are cheap and simple. Here is what works, starting with the free options.

Paper and pencil: still the most reliable method

Print the pattern (or copy the page, so your book stays clean) and work from the copy. Then:

  • Cross off each row as you finish it. Use pencil, so you can erase if you have to rip back.
  • Draw an arrow at the row you are on before you put the work down, and circle the very next step.
  • Keep a tally on a sticky note or index card for plain sections like "work even for 30 rows", where there is no written line to cross off.
  • Break long patterns into sections and cross off each section when it is done, so you are only ever looking at a small piece.

If you want a ready-made sheet, our free printable row counter has 120 numbered row boxes, repeat circles and stitch count checks on one page.

Sticky notes, highlighter tape and a ruler

To keep your eyes on the right line, cover the rows you are not working on. Many knitters place a long sticky note or a ruler just above or below the current line and move it down as they go. Removable highlighter tape does the same job and lets you still read the line through it. A magnetic board with a strip magnet is a sturdier version of the same idea that several forum knitters use.

One habit from the forum is worth copying on its own: try to finish the row before you put your work down. If you always stop at the end of a row, you always know where the next session begins.

Stitch markers for repeats inside a row

Row counts are only half the problem. The other half is losing your place partway across a long row of repeats. Stitch markers solve this. According to Yarnspirations, they come in three common types: closed rings (or a loop of contrasting yarn), split-ring markers, and locking markers that look like small safety pins.

  • Place a marker between pattern repeats, so each section between markers should look the same. If a section looks wrong, you only have to check that one.
  • Make your beginning-of-round marker different in color or shape from the rest, so you never confuse it with a repeat marker.
  • On very long rows, add a marker every 25 stitches or so so you can recount in small chunks instead of from the start.
  • Use a locking marker to flag a row. Locking and split markers can be clipped into the fabric itself, so you can mark the row where you last decreased, or the right side of the work, and remove it later.

Crocheters get the same benefit. The authors of Crocheting For Dummies note that stitches are often lost at the start or end of a row, and suggest a removable marker in the first and last stitch of the row so you know exactly where to work (Dummies).

Lifelines: a safety net for long or difficult work

A lifeline does not track your place; it protects it. It is a piece of smooth, thin, contrasting yarn (some people use plain dental floss) threaded through every stitch of a row you know is correct. If you later find a mistake, you can rip back to the lifeline and the unraveling stops there, so you can put the stitches back on the needle without dropping or twisting them. Yarnspirations suggests placing one about every 10 rows in a difficult lace pattern (Yarnspirations: How to Use a Lifeline).

  1. Knit to the row you want to save. Count your stitches and check the pattern first.
  2. Thread a yarn needle with a length of lifeline yarn longer than your work is wide.
  3. Run the needle through every live stitch on your knitting needle, then remove the yarn needle and leave the lifeline in place.
  4. Carry on knitting as normal, working past the lifeline.

Write the row number of each lifeline on your pattern. Then, if you ever rip back, you know exactly which row you are restarting from.

Learn to count rows in the fabric itself

Every method above can fail. A sticky note falls off, a tally gets smudged, someone tidies up your pattern. The one record that cannot be lost is the knitting itself, so it pays to learn to read it.

  • Stockinette: on the right side, each knit stitch looks like a V. Count up one column of Vs; each V is one row (Brooklyn Tweed, Berroco).
  • Fuzzy or multicolored yarn: try the wrong side, where each row is a line of purl bumps that can be easier to see. Gently stretching the fabric a little can separate the rows (Berroco).
  • Garter stitch: rows of Vs alternate with rows of bumps, so count a V, then the bumps above it, then the next V, and so on (Berroco).
  • Do not count the loops on your needle. They are not a finished row yet (Berroco's design team, answering a reader in the comments).

A good habit: at the end of each section, count the rows in your fabric and compare them with your paper or counter. If they disagree, trust the fabric and fix the record. Some stitch patterns, like open lace, are hard to read in the fabric; for those, the Berroco design team suggests tracking on paper or with a row counter instead.

Row counters: physical and digital

A simple row counter takes the counting out of your head. Options include a counter that slips onto your needle, a counter app on your phone, and the "rosary" or linked-marker chains some knitters make and use as a row counter, which forum knitters recommend for repeats like decreasing every few rows.

Whatever you use, the rule is the same: update the count the moment you finish the row, before you look at your phone or put the work down. Most counting mistakes happen when you tell yourself you will remember.

Tracking two things at once

The trickiest patterns ask you to track two counts at the same time, such as "repeat rows 1 to 8 six times" or "increase every 4th row while working the lace pattern". A few ways to keep both straight:

  • Use two counters: one for the row of the repeat (1 to 8) and one for how many repeats you have finished.
  • On paper, write the repeat out as a small grid, one box per row and one line per repeat, and tick boxes as you go.
  • Clip a locking marker into the fabric on each shaping row, so you can count back to the last one at a glance.

The one thing to take away

Choose a method you will actually use, keep the count in one place, and update it the moment each row is done. Back that up with markers for repeats and the ability to count rows in your fabric, and losing your place becomes a small fix instead of an evening of ripping back.
