Crochet Stitch Count Wrong? How to Find the Problem and Fix It

By Will McCullough, DigiRune Studios · November 23, 2026

The short answer: most of the time the pattern is fine and the difference is at the edges of the row. Recount, then check whether your turning chain should count as a stitch, and whether you worked into the first and last stitch the way the pattern expects. If the count is still off, check the pattern's own math, whether it uses US or UK terms, and whether the designer has published a correction. Only then decide whether to rip back or quietly fix it on the next row.

You reach the end of a row, count, and get 31 when the pattern says 30. Or worse, row 14 asks for a number that does not seem possible from row 13. Even a beginner's practice swatch can raise it (see this KnittingHelp thread, where a new crocheter ends up with 11 posts instead of 10). Work through the checks below in order. The early ones fix most problems.

1. Recount, carefully

Count the tops of the stitches (the little V shapes along the top of the row), or run your thumb along the posts and count each one. A few things to keep in mind:

  • The loop on your hook never counts as a stitch (Sigoni Macaroni).
  • The foundation chain is not a row. Your first row is the first row of stitches worked into it (Sigoni Macaroni).
  • On long rows, place a stitch marker every 10 stitches and count the groups. It is much easier to recount 10 than 60.

Count twice. If you get two different numbers, count a third time before you change anything.

2. Check the turning chain

Sigoni Macaroni calls whether the turning chain counts the most frequently asked crochet question, and it is a common reason a count is off by one. The turning chain brings your hook up to the height of the next row's stitches. Lion Brand gives the usual lengths (Lion Brand glossary):

Stitch (US terms)Turning chain
Single crochet1 chain
Half double crochet2 chains
Double crochet3 chains
Treble (triple) crochet4 chains

Whether that chain counts as a stitch depends on the stitch and on the designer:

  • Taller stitches (usually): the turning chain takes the place of the first stitch. You skip the first stitch at the base, and at the end of the next row you work into the top of the turning chain, treating it as a stitch (Lion Brand).
  • Half double crochet is a common exception: Sigoni Macaroni notes that its chain-2 usually does not count unless the pattern says it does.
  • Single crochet: the chain-1 does not count. You work your first single crochet into the first stitch, and you do not work into that chain on the return row (Lion Brand).
  • Designers differ. Some patterns state that the turning chain does not count as a stitch even for taller stitches. Look for a note at the start of the pattern or at the start of each row, and follow the pattern over any general rule (Sigoni Macaroni).

How this shows up in your count:

  • One stitch too many: the chain counts as a stitch, but you also worked into the first stitch at the base.
  • One stitch too few: the chain counts as a stitch, but you did not work into it at the end of the row.

3. Check the first and last stitch

Even when you have the turning chain right, the edge stitches are easy to miss. The authors of Crocheting For Dummies point out that stitches are commonly lost at the beginning or end of a row because those stitches look different from the rest, and suggest clipping a removable stitch marker into the first and last stitch of each row so you know exactly where to work on the way back (Dummies). If your work is slowly getting narrower or wider, you have lost or added stitches somewhere, and the edges are a common place for it (Dummies).

4. Check the pattern's math

If your stitches are right, test the pattern line itself. Most row instructions can be checked with simple arithmetic. Count what each repeat uses and makes:

Example (a made-up row for practice): the round before has 18 stitches. The instruction is "*sc in next 2 sts, 2 sc in next st; rep from * around (24 sts)". Each repeat uses 3 stitches and makes 4. 18 stitches divided by 3 is 6 repeats, and 6 repeats times 4 is 24. The count checks out.

Now try it when it does not. If the round before had 20 stitches, 20 does not divide evenly by 3, so that line cannot be worked exactly as written. That points either to a mistake in an earlier row (yours or the pattern's) or to an error in the pattern.

  • Check the stitch count at the end of each row, usually shown in brackets or after a dash. Does the previous row's count lead to this one?
  • Check whether a row starts or ends with stitches outside the repeat, such as "sc in first 2 sts, *...* to last st, sc in last st". Those count too.
  • If the pattern has sizes, make sure you are reading the number for your size every time.

5. Check US or UK terms

The same stitch names mean different stitches in US and UK patterns. A US double crochet is a UK treble, and a UK double crochet is a US single crochet (Craft Yarn Council). Using the wrong set will not always change your stitch count, but it changes the height of most stitches, so rows come out taller or shorter than intended and the piece can look badly wrong. If the pattern does not say which terms it uses, look for clues: a UK pattern usually has no "single crochet" at all. Our free US and UK crochet terms chart has the full list side by side.

Also check the pattern's own abbreviations list. The Craft Yarn Council notes that designers may use special abbreviations, usually defined at the start of the book or pattern.

6. Look for errata

Patterns are written by people, and mistakes do get printed. A correction is called errata. Where to look:

  • The pattern's page on the publisher's or designer's website, or wherever you bought it. Knit Picks, for example, notes errata on the pattern's page and sends updated copies to the pattern library of buyers who saved it there (Knit Picks).
  • The book publisher's corrections page, for patterns from a book.
  • The designer directly. Knit Picks, for example, sends questions on its independent-designer patterns to the designer (Knit Picks). Send the row number, the count you expected and the count you got; a clear, polite note helps.

7. Decide: rip back or fix it on the next row

Once you know where the count went wrong, you have two honest options.

  • Rip back. Pull out rows one at a time, counting as you go, until you reach a row with the right count, then work forward again (Dummies). This is the right choice if the mistake would show, or if it throws off a stitch pattern.
  • Fix it on the next row. If you are only one or two stitches off in plain fabric, Crocheting For Dummies suggests working two stitches together to remove an extra stitch, or two stitches in one stitch to add a missing one, until the count is right again, then checking that the edges still look even (Dummies).

To stop it happening again, count your stitches at the end of every row until it becomes second nature, and keep markers in the first and last stitches.
